# ICP Communication Playbook — How Lorinczi Talks About Redesign
_Research-backed guide for all copy, consultations, email, DMs, and social. Built from a 23-source deep-research pass (grief psychology, consumer research, grief-marketing ethics); every claim below survived 3-vote adversarial verification. Created 2026-07-25._

_Companion to the 4-ICP framework: (1) upgrade couples, (2) divorced women, (3) widowed women, (4) heirloom inheritors._

---

## The core stance (if you read nothing else)

**Redesign is carrying a bond forward in a new form — it is never closure.**

Modern grief science (continuing-bonds theory, Klass/Silverman/Nickman; 2023 *Death Studies* systematic review) established that maintaining an ongoing inner relationship with someone who died — through memories, rituals, and physical objects — is a *normal, healthy* part of grieving. The "let go and move on" model it replaced descends from discredited early-Freudian ideas. Grief experts now flag **"closure," "letting go," "moving on," and the Five Stages as outdated** — and grieving people themselves report these phrases as hurtful.

This is extraordinary luck for Lorinczi: **what Stacey does — same stones, same gold, new form — is the physical embodiment of what the science says healthy grief looks like.** The marketing job is not to invent a story; it's to describe the work accurately and never contaminate it with the closure language every competitor uses.

Three hard rules sit above everything:

1. **Never promise healing.** No study shows a redesigned piece heals grief; research explicitly finds bonds through objects bring *both comfort and distress*. Copy stays descriptive — "many clients tell me…" — never clinical — "this will help you heal." (One study even found early comfort-seeking through a deceased spouse's possessions predicted *more severe* grief — one more reason to never imply urgency.)
2. **The client owns the timeline and the choice. Always.** No deadlines, no "when you're ready to move forward," no anniversary hooks, no implication that redesigning is the "healthy" option and keeping the ring untouched isn't. Practitioner gold standard (Jeanette Walker): *"There is no right or wrong protocol. I suggest doing whatever feels right for you."*
3. **Never use proof-of-love framing.** The documented exploitation pattern in grief industries (funeral-home research): guilt-based upsells — *"Don't you want the best for your loved one?"* Lorinczi never links spend, or the decision to redesign at all, to how much someone loved. This is the line between us and the grief-industrial complex.

**The ghost-sentence filter (use on every sentence):** grieving people hear an implied *"…so don't feel so bad"* at the end of platitudes. If a sentence still works with "so don't feel so bad" appended — cut it. ("She's in a better place *so don't feel so bad*." "At least you had 30 years *so don't feel so bad*.")

## The science in five concepts

| Concept | What it says | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuing bonds** (Klass et al.; Hewson 2023 review) | Ongoing inner relationships with the dead are normal, expressed partly through objects | Frame: "carry forward," "a new chapter of the same story" — never "move on" |
| **Linking objects** (Volkan) | Objects frozen as magical talismans mark *complicated* mourning; adaptive bonds are internalized and transformed | Transformation aligns with the healthy pattern — but we never diagnose, and never say keeping a ring unchanged is unhealthy |
| **Extended self** (Belk 1988) | Possessions are experienced as literal parts of the self; a dead spouse's possessions are "powerful remains of the dead person's extended self" | Handle pieces with *stated* reverence: who touches them (only Stacey), how they're safeguarded. The consultation is identity work, not a transaction: "what story should this piece tell going forward?" |
| **Self-loss & disenfranchised grief** (Parkes; Doka) | Losing a spouse — by death *or divorce* — is felt as losing part of yourself ("as if half of myself was missing"); ex-spouse grief is real but socially unrecognized | Widows: acknowledge the loss touches who they *are*. Divorcées: validate that grieving a marriage is legitimate even when the world says "be glad it's over" |
| **Memory-distance** (Madon 2023; McAlexander's divorce research) | Separation drives some people to shed objects *because* they hold too many memories ("I left everything behind… I don't like memories very much") | Divorce copy must offer **two honest paths** — dismantle the old form entirely (melt, reclaim value, new piece) or rework what was good — and let *her* name which. Never assume sentimentality |

## Per-ICP playbook

### ICP 1 — Upgrade couples (celebration register)
- **Mindset:** pride, milestone, "we made it." Low grief; the caution rules above mostly don't apply. Standard celebratory framing is right.
- **One watch-item (unverified, flagged by research as open):** *upgrade guilt* — disloyalty to the original ring. Preempt gently: the original stones/metal can live on inside the upgrade ("your original diamond, at the center of what it became").
- **Say:** celebrate, milestone, the next chapter *of the same marriage*, "the ring your life grew into."
- **Avoid:** anything that disparages the original ring ("finally get the ring you deserve" implies the first one was a compromise *and* insults the partner who chose it).

### ICP 2 — Divorced women (reclamation register)
- **Mindset:** identity reconstruction. The ring is memory-saturated; some want its meaning honored, some want it *dismantled*. Her grief is disenfranchised — people expect relief, she may feel loss, relief, anger, and love at once.
- **Frame:** authorship of who she's becoming. "The diamond was never the marriage." Redesign as a *synthesizing* move: old material, new self.
- **Always offer both paths without judging either:** (a) melt it down, erase the old form, reclaim the value into something entirely new; (b) rework it into something that honors what was good. Ask which — don't assume.
- **Say:** "yours, entirely," "designed around you this time," "whatever you feel about this ring — including nothing — is allowed."
- **Avoid:** assuming sentimentality; assuming she wants to keep anything; celebration framing ("treat yourself!"); "fresh start" as a *prescription*. "Close the chapter" is acceptable here **only** as one option she might choose, never as the recommendation.

### ICP 3 — Widowed women (memorial register — the most protected space)
- **Mindset:** the ring is part of him and part of her (extended self). Ambivalence is normal and documented: comfort *and* distress from the same object. Society pressures her with timelines from both directions (wear it forever / time to move on) — both are prescriptions, both are wrong.
- **Frame:** continuing the bond in a form she can carry. "The sentiment stays; the form changes." Two rings becoming one piece = the bond made physical.
- **Lead with agency:** "You don't have to decide anything yet" is the single most trustworthy sentence we can say — it's also what no funeral-adjacent marketer says, which is why it reads as authentic.
- **Say:** "there is no timeline," "the rings will wait, and so will I," "whatever you feel — love, grief, even relief — is allowed here," "handled by exactly one person: me."
- **Avoid (absolute):** closure, moving on, letting go, healing, "at least…", "she'd want you to…", any anniversary/timing hook, any suggestion that redesigning is the *healthy* choice, any urgency whatsoever.

### ICP 4 — Heirloom inheritors (legacy register)
- **Mindset:** custodianship + guilt. The piece is "powerful remains" of the person, but it sits in a drawer — and *that* makes them feel guilty too. They need permission: transforming it is honoring it, not destroying it.
- **Frame:** continuity of story. Practitioner gold standard (Kate Rose): *"The sentimental value doesn't disappear. It just moves into a form you can carry with you."* A drawer keeps the object; a redesign keeps the *relationship*.
- **Say:** "her stones, worn again," "the story continues," "jewelry is meant to be worn — she wore hers."
- **Avoid:** "unlock the value," "modernize" as the lead frame (it implies the original is obsolete — the *person* chose that piece), scolding the drawer ("don't let it gather dust" shames them).

## Authenticity principles (the "humble, graceful, respectful" operating rules)

1. **Describe, don't perform.** Authenticity = specificity. "Widows have sat at my bench for twenty years" is credible; "we understand your pain" is performed empathy from nobody in particular. First-person singular is Lorinczi's structural advantage — a "we" can't be humble, a person can.
2. **Witness, don't fix.** Stacey's role in a consultation is to hear the story and show options — not to counsel, diagnose, or promise emotional outcomes. Invite the story as *design input* ("tell me as much or as little as you want — it shapes what I make"), which is honest, not extractive.
3. **Every CTA is concrete, small, and reversible.** Research: specific low-pressure offers preserve autonomy; vague ones ("reach out anytime") burden people. Model: "a free conversation — no commitment, and the rings stay untouched until you decide." Always include the exit: "if you decide to change nothing, that's a good outcome too."
4. **Name what happens to the object.** Because the piece is experienced as part of a person: who handles it (one person), how it ships (insured, tracked), what happens to every gram of the metal. Reverence is logistics, stated plainly.
5. **Let clients say the emotional things.** In testimonials and case studies, the *client* says "I felt him with me again" — Stacey never claims it on their behalf. With grief clients: always ask permission before featuring a story, and offer anonymity.
6. **No manufactured urgency, ever, on any surface.** No countdown timers, no "book this month," no seasonal grief hooks ("this Mother's Day, honor her memory with…" is the exploitation pattern). Demand here is permanent; patience *is* the marketing.

## Surface application

- **Web copy:** question-as-H2s in the client's own words; ambivalence named; both-paths offered (divorce); agency lead (widow). ✅ *The two live drafts follow this — audited below.*
- **Consultations:** open with the story invitation; present options in pairs (change nothing / change form); end by handing the decision back with no follow-up pressure. A "no" costs nothing and earns referrals in grief communities.
- **Email:** one concrete next step per email; never automated nurture-pressure sequences for ICP 2/3 inquiries — a single gentle check-in ("no rush from my side — whenever you're ready, or never, is fine") then silence until they move.
- **Social:** before/after stories with permission; the client's words in quotes; never post a grief transformation as content without explicit sign-off. Celebration register (ICP 1) can be promotional; memorial register (ICP 3) never is.
- **Reviews/GBP:** review prompts may reference the piece ("redesigned my mother's ring") — never prompt for emotional testimony.

## Draft-page audit (against this playbook)

Both live drafts pass the blacklist clean (no closure/moving-on/at-least/timeline language; agency leads; reversible CTAs). Two refinements applied from the research:
1. **Widow page:** added explicit ambivalence permission — research shows the same ring brings comfort *and* distress, and naming that is what the literature says helps.
2. **Divorce page:** added validation that grieving a marriage is legitimate (disenfranchised grief) alongside the existing both-paths structure.

## Known limits (honesty about the evidence)

- All mappings from grief research to *jewelry marketing* are reasonable application, not tested findings — no study has examined jewelry redesign as a grief intervention. Hence: descriptive copy only, forever.
- No verified findings surfaced on ethical-marketing *codes* for memorial jewelry specifically, or on upgrade guilt (ICP 1) — both flagged as open questions.
- Timing is genuinely unknown: no evidence on when in a grief trajectory redesign tends to be experienced as right vs. premature. One more reason the client sets the pace.
