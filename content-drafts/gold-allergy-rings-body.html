IS IT REALLY THE GOLD?

Usually not. Pure gold is one of the least reactive metals there is, which is why it has been worn against skin for thousands of years. But pure gold is also far too soft to hold a stone, so every piece of jewelry gold is an alloy — and the other metals in that mix are where trouble tends to start.

The usual suspect is nickel. It is the most common contact allergen there is, and it has long been used to make white gold both white and hard. Copper is the next one worth knowing about — it's what gives rose gold its color.

Two things follow from that, and between them they explain most of the pattern people describe to me:

  • Karat matters. 14k gold is 58.5% gold; 18k is 75%. The lower the karat, the more room there is for whatever else is in the mix. A ring that bothers you in 14k may be perfectly comfortable in 18k.
  • Color matters. White gold is the most common offender, because of the nickel. Rose gold carries the most copper. Yellow gold at the same karat is often the mildest of the three.

It's also why people tell me one ring bothers them and another doesn't, even though both are "gold." They are not the same alloy.

IT MIGHT NOT BE AN ALLERGY AT ALL

Worth ruling out before you spend anything. A ring traps water, soap, and hand lotion against your skin all day, and moisture plus friction can produce a red, sore, peeling band that looks exactly like an allergic reaction and isn't one. It usually shows up after something changes — a new soap, a hotter summer, a ring that has grown a little tight.

If that's what's happening, taking the ring off for a couple of weeks and drying carefully underneath it tends to settle things, and no new metal is required. I would much rather tell you that than sell you a platinum setting you didn't need.

I'M A JEWELER, NOT A DOCTOR

I want to be straight about the limits of what I know. I can tell you what metals are in a ring and what they tend to do. I cannot tell you what your skin is reacting to, and neither can anyone else who is selling you jewelry.

If this has been going on a while, a dermatologist can patch test you and give you a real answer — including which metal, which is exactly the thing that decides what your piece should be made in. That test costs a fraction of a redesign, and it means you're solving the right problem. If you'd like to do that first and come back to me afterward, that is genuinely the order I'd choose myself.

WHICH METALS ARE LEAST LIKELY TO BOTHER YOU

Assuming metal really is the issue, this is how I'd think about it:

  • Platinum. The usual answer, and what most people in this situation end up choosing. Jewelry platinum is typically 95% pure, so there is very little else in it to react to. It's denser and more durable than gold, and it holds stones beautifully. It does cost more, and I'll be specific about that below.
  • Higher-karat yellow gold. 18k or above. Less alloy, by definition. A reasonable option if you love the warmth of gold and the reaction is mild.
  • Palladium white gold. White gold made white with palladium instead of nickel. Better for many nickel-sensitive people — though palladium causes reactions in some, so it isn't a guarantee.

What I won't promise you is that any metal is universally safe. People react to different things, which is the whole reason the patch test is worth doing.

WHAT I'D ACTUALLY DO WITH YOUR RING

Here's the encouraging part: the expensive half of your ring has nothing to do with the problem. Diamonds and gemstones are not what your skin is reacting to. So the work is simply to move your stones out of the metal that bothers you and into metal that doesn't.

That tends to go one of two ways, and both are common:

  • Replicate it. If you love the ring as it is, I can rebuild the same design in platinum. It will still look like your ring, because it still is your ring — same stones, same shape, different metal.

WHAT HAPPENS TO THE GOLD YOU CAN'T WEAR

It doesn't vanish into my drawer. I refine the old gold — including the metal in the settings I'm removing your stones from — and apply its full settlement value as a credit toward the new piece. Honestly, it depends on the setting, and platinum is the more expensive metal. Some ranges to work from:

  • A setting designed from scratch around your stones: $1,500–$3,500.
  • Choosing platinum over gold: adds roughly $500–$1,100, depending on the finished weight of the piece.
