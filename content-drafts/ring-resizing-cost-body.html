HOW MUCH DOES IT COST TO RESIZE A RING?

These are the ranges you should expect in 2026, for a competent bench jeweler:

  • Simple sizing down (plain band, gold): $40–$80. The cheapest thing you can ask for.
  • Simple sizing up (plain band, gold): $80–$150. More, always — and the next section explains why.
  • Sterling silver: $20–$60. Silver is soft and quick to work.
  • Platinum: $50–$150, and often half again as much as the equivalent gold job. Platinum is wonderful, durable stuff, and it is genuinely harder to work — it needs higher heat and more patience.
  • Rings with pavé, side stones, or engraving: $150–$300 and up. You're not paying for the sizing, you're paying for the care it takes to protect everything else while it happens.

One local note, since most of my clients are here: the Bay Area runs about 15–25% above the national average on bench labor. That's not anyone gouging you, it's what it costs to keep a bench in San Francisco.

And a caveat I'd want if I were you: these are market ranges, not my prices. Get a quote from the person actually doing the work, and get it before they start.

WHY DOES SIZING UP COST MORE THAN SIZING DOWN?

Because of the metal. To make a ring smaller, the jeweler cuts out a section, brings the ends together, and solders it — the metal that comes out is scrap they keep. To make it larger, they have to cut the shank and add a piece of matching metal, which they have to buy. With gold where it is right now, that difference is real money.

It's the same reason I ask clients about refining rather than melting: metal has value, and the direction it flows matters.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS WHEN A RING IS RESIZED

There are really only two things a jeweler can do: take metal out, or put metal in. Sizing down means cutting a section from the shank, bringing the ends back together and soldering the join. Sizing up means cutting the shank and letting in a piece of matching metal — or, for a small increase on a plain band, stretching the metal instead.

Stretching is where I'd want you to pay attention. It's quicker and cheaper, and for a plain band going up a fraction of a size it's perfectly reasonable. But stretching doesn't create metal out of nothing — it thins the shank, and it pulls on everything mounted to it.

Which brings me to the part that matters most and gets mentioned least. When the metal moves, the settings move with it. Every prong, every bead, every bezel is holding its stone under tension, and that tension was set when the ring was its original size. Stretch or compress the shank and you change the geometry those settings were built around. So the work isn't finished when the ring is the right size — it's finished when every stone has been checked and tightened again and the tension is genuinely back where it should be.

That's the real reason a stone-set ring costs more to size than a plain band, and it's why "how many sizes" is the first question I ask. Half a size is a small adjustment. Three sizes is a different job, involving real movement in metal that has stones depending on it.

IS RESIZING A PLATINUM RING DIFFERENT FROM GOLD?

Yes, genuinely — and it's the reason platinum costs more, rather than jewelers simply charging a premium for the word.

Gold is soldered with a torch. It's straightforward bench work, and a good jeweler does it every week. Platinum melts at close to 1,770°C against gold's 1,060°C or so, which means it needs platinum solder at far higher heat, or it needs a laser.

Laser welding is the part worth understanding, because it changes what's possible. A laser puts heat exactly where you point it and almost nowhere else — you can weld within a millimeter or two of a set stone without that heat traveling through the metal to reach it. On a torch, that same join means the heat spreads, and anything sensitive nearby has to come out first.

So for a platinum ring with stones still set, a laser is often the difference between resizing it safely and having to unset and reset everything. It's also what I'd want for any ring holding emeralds, opals, pearls, or turquoise, whatever the metal — those stones don't forgive a torch.

Not every bench has one. If your ring is platinum, or it holds heat-sensitive stones, that's a fair thing to ask before you leave it with anyone: do you laser weld, or torch? Neither answer is wrong on its own — but the answer should match your ring.

CAN EVERY RING BE RESIZED?

No — and the test is simpler than most people expect. Is there plain, unset metal at the bottom of the shank? That's the whole question. That plain section is where a jeweler cuts. If it's there, you have options. If stones run all the way around, there's nowhere to cut without going through the structure that holds them.

So: a plain band, or a solitaire with a smooth shank, is straightforward. A ring with pavé partway down the sides can usually move a little. A full eternity band generally can't be sized at all.

WHY CAN'T ETERNITY BANDS BE RESIZED?

Because the stones are the band. There's no quiet stretch of metal to open up, and cutting into it means cutting through the settings themselves. Push a shank like that even slightly and the tension holding each stone changes — beads loosen, stones sit crooked, and eventually something falls out. Most jewelers will decline, and the ones who say yes are the ones I'd worry about.

With a full eternity band your realistic options are to wear it on a different finger, or to have it remade at your size using your own stones. That second one is my kind of work, and it's a bigger conversation than a resize.

HOW MANY SIZES CAN A RING SAFELY MOVE?

Less than people hope. A plain band can travel a couple of sizes without complaint. Once there are stones set into the shank, you're realistically looking at half a size to one size — flexing the shank flexes the tiny beads holding each accent stone, and there's only so much give before they let go.

Beyond about two sizes, even on a plain band, you start thinning and stressing the shank where it was joined. It'll look fine on the day. I'm more interested in whether it's still fine in twenty years, which is a different question and the one I'd ask about any piece I made.

DOES RESIZING DAMAGE A RING?

Done well, no. A clean cut, matched metal, a proper solder, a shank that's the same thickness it started as, and every stone checked and re-tightened afterwards — done that way you'd never know. Ask to see the inside of the shank when you collect it. A well-made join is almost invisible, and if you can spot it easily, so can everyone else.

Done badly, you get a visible seam, a shank left thin where it was stretched, or stones sitting a little looser than they did — which is the one you won't notice until something falls out months later. That's why the checking afterwards matters as much as the sizing itself.

The one thing I'd genuinely warn you about: heat. Sizing means a torch near your stones. Diamonds are fine. Emeralds, opals, pearls, turquoise and most other colored stones are not — they can crack, cloud, or lose color. A careful jeweler removes them first and resets them after, and that's part of why a stone-set resize costs what it does. If a jeweler doesn't mention this, find another jeweler.

WHEN RESIZING ISN'T REALLY THE ANSWER

Here's the conversation I actually have most often, and the reason this page exists.

A lot of the rings that don't fit are rings that aren't being worn anyway. They were inherited from a mother or grandmother. They're from a marriage that ended. They were bought for a version of you from twenty years ago. And the ring not fitting is simply the thing that finally prompted a decision.

If that's you, it's worth asking a different question before you spend anything. Not what does it cost to make this fit, but would I wear this if it did?

Because if the answer is no, resizing is money spent on a ring that goes back in the drawer at a slightly better size. Whereas the diamond and the gold in that ring are the two most expensive parts of any piece of jewelry, and you already own them. Reset the stone into something you'd actually reach for and you keep everything that mattered — the stone, the metal, the history — and lose only the shape you'd stopped loving.

WHAT ABOUT A RING THAT WON'T CLEAR MY KNUCKLE?

This one has a solution most people don't know about. If your knuckle is larger than the base of your finger — which happens with arthritis, and happens to plenty of us simply with time; my own knuckles have grown — a ring sized to slide over the knuckle will spin loosely once it's on.

The fix is a fitted shank: a hinged shank that opens, or sizing beads added inside the band. Both let the ring pass the knuckle and then sit snugly. Any good bench jeweler can advise, and it's worth asking about specifically, because a plain resize won't solve it and you'll end up back where you started.
